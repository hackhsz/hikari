# 10 — Borrowed Imagery: what the look is, and how to get it legally

> **The question asked:** the reference account puts jewellery onto oil paintings and old photographs and it looks superb. Does posting that on Instagram rather than the product site avoid infringement? And can the backdrop be AI-generated instead?

**None of this is legal advice.** It is reasoned from general principles in the same way [06](06-unit-economics.md) and [09](09-payments-and-tax.md) reason about tax, and it carries the same caveat: confirm with a UK IP solicitor before relying on it. The hour is cheap ([04](04-site-ia.md) already budgets for it).

---

## Short answers

**Does Instagram-only help?** No — and it is arguably the *worse* surface, not the safer one.

**Can AI generate the backdrop instead?** It solves the third-party copyright problem and creates three others, one of which cuts against the core of this business.

**Is the look achievable legally?** Yes, completely, and by four different routes — one of which produces a *better* image than the reference's, not merely a lawful one. That is the actual finding here and it is where the rest of this document goes.

---

## 1 · Why "only on Instagram" doesn't work

The instinct is that the product page is where money changes hands, so the product page is where the exposure is. That is not how copyright works.

**The act that infringes is the copying and the communication to the public.** Posting the image publicly is that act. It happens on Instagram in full. Whether a sale is transacted on the same surface goes to *damages*, potentially, and not to *liability*.

Three reasons Instagram is in fact the more exposed surface:

- **It is more public, not less.** A business account posting to 20k people, indexed, shareable, screenshotted. A product page is seen by people who already found you.
- **The UK has no "fair use".** It has *fair dealing*, which is a **closed list** of purposes: research and private study, criticism, review, quotation, news reporting, parody, caricature and pastiche. "It suited my shop's aesthetic" is not on the list, and the commercial context makes the remaining arguments worse rather than better.
- **You warrant the rights when you post.** Instagram's terms require you to hold the rights to what you upload. Using someone else's photograph breaches the platform contract independently of breaching the photographer's copyright.

### The risk that actually matters is not being sued

Realistically, a small UK reseller is unlikely to be sued by a photo agency over one post. **That is not the risk.** The risk is the takedown mechanism:

> A rightsholder files a notice. The post goes. Repeat notices strike the account. Enough strikes and the account is gone — **and with it the entire primary channel**, the followers, the archive, the Edition ritual, and the handle.

[05](05-social.md) makes Instagram the primary channel. [D1](DECISIONS.md) makes the owned site the home precisely so the business does not sit on rented land — but the *audience* still does. **An account built over two years can be removed in an afternoon by a stock agency's automated matching system, and there is no insurance for it.** Agency photographs, which is what the reference's black-and-white fashion image almost certainly is, are exactly the material these systems are best at finding.

Weighed against a backdrop that four legal routes below supply anyway, the trade is bad on its own terms.

## 2 · The two borrowed images are not the same risk

They were treated as one problem in [05](05-social.md). They are not.

### The black-and-white fashion photograph — the serious one

Almost certainly still in copyright: photographs run for the photographer's life plus 70 years, so a 1980s or 1990s editorial shot has decades left. There may also be a **model release** question — a living, identifiable person used to promote a commercial product, which raises data protection and, if it implies endorsement, passing off. There is no route to using this image. It is simply someone else's work.

### The oil painting — depends entirely on facts the post doesn't state

**The painting itself** is public domain if the artist died more than 70 years ago (UK and EU: life + 70). If it is a contemporary painter working in a classical style — which, on the evidence of the image, is entirely possible — it is fully in copyright and no better than the photograph.

**The photograph *of* the painting** is the trap that catches people who think they have cleared the first hurdle. A faithful reproduction of a public-domain artwork is unlikely to attract fresh copyright in the UK, on the reasoning that it lacks the originality required for a work to be its author's own intellectual creation — but museums and picture libraries routinely assert rights anyway, and many bind you **contractually** through their website terms even where the copyright claim is weak. A contract claim does not care that the underlying painting is free.

**This is the whole reason route A below specifies open-access museum sources rather than image search.** The problem is not finding an old painting; it is being able to *prove* what you used and under what terms.

## 3 · AI-generating the backdrop

It removes the third-party copyright problem in the source image, which is real and is the biggest single win available. It then introduces three things:

**You may not own the result.** The UK is unusual in providing for computer-generated works with no human author (50 years, authorship attributed to the person who made the arrangements necessary for its creation), but the provision is contested, under review, and untested for modern generative output. In most other jurisdictions, material with no human author simply has no copyright. **For a brand whose only durable asset at Phase 1 is a visual identity, "a competitor can lift the entire feed and you have no claim" is a material downside**, not a technicality.

**Prompting toward a specific look can land you back where you started.** Asking for a named artist's style, or for something close to a particular photograph, can produce output substantially similar to a protected work. The copyright question follows the *output*, not the method.

**Advertising rules apply to your own posts.** In the UK the CAP Code covers a brand's own social content, and it requires that marketing communications not mislead. An imagined backdrop is unlikely to trouble anyone. An image that misrepresents *the product* — its scale, colour, condition, how it sits on a real ear — is a different matter, and it is the same failure mode as the composites in [05](05-social.md).

### The objection specific to this business

[08](08-brand.md) already moved the palette off warm cream + high-contrast serif + terracotta for a stated reason: it is **a recognisable signature of AI-generated design**, and a brand arguing that every competitor looks alike cannot afford to look generated.

Putting actual generated imagery in the feed contradicts that decision far more loudly than a hex value ever did. And it does so in the one category where it costs the most: [03](03-customer.md) establishes that **the barrier is trust, not price**, and that the customer's blocker is *"is this site real?"*. Hikari sells authenticity — real object, real age, real provenance, *found in Tokyo, March 2026*. A feed whose atmosphere is synthetic is arguing against its own product, and the audience for vintage is unusually quick to spot it.

**Being caught once costs more than the images were ever worth.** Not recommended, and if used at all, never for anything touching the object.

## 4 · What the look actually is

The useful move is to stop calling it "an oil painting" and name the mechanism, because once named it is obviously reproducible.

Both reference images do the same four things:

| | The mechanism |
|---|---|
| **1** | **A human presence that is not a modern product shot** — a painted neck, a grainy profile. It says *worn*, not *sold*. |
| **2** | **Texture and depth behind the object** — brushwork, film grain. The object sits *in* something rather than on a sweep. |
| **3** | **Time collapse.** A 1985 object inside a much older frame. This is what does the "timeless" work the caption claims. |
| **4** | **The jewellery is the only sharp, saturated, real thing** in a soft or monochrome field. All attention lands on it. |

**Not one of those four requires someone else's picture.** The reference borrowed because borrowing was easy, not because the effect demanded it.

## 5 · Four routes to the same mechanism, ranked

### Route A · Shoot it physically against a verified public-domain painting — **recommended**

Not a composite. **Print the painting and photograph the piece against it, in one real exposure.**

Source from open-access museum programmes, which publish high resolution with the licence stated on the record: **the Metropolitan Museum** (CC0), the **Rijksmuseum**, the **Art Institute of Chicago** (CC0), the **National Gallery of Art, Washington**, **Getty Open Content**, the **Smithsonian** (CC0). These exist precisely so that the status is documented rather than inferred, which is the whole problem in §2. A large-format print costs £20–40 and is reusable indefinitely.

Shooting it rather than compositing it wins four times over:

- **The copyright question is closed** at the source, with a licence record you can produce.
- **You own the resulting photograph outright** — it is your original work, which is exactly what §3 cannot give you.
- **The light is real**, so it is one coherent image. Composite artefacts — mismatched light direction, wrong edge quality, impossible shadows — are visible to anyone with an eye, and they read as cheap the moment they are noticed.
- **It keeps the evidence intact.** Real scale, real depth, real condition. It does not break frame 3's job ([08](08-brand.md)) the way a pasted-on earring does.

### Route B · Commission the painting

An illustrator or painter, work-for-hire, **copyright assigned in writing** (in the UK, assignment must be in writing and signed — commissioning alone does not transfer it). Roughly £200–800 for a portrait-scale piece.

Expensive once, then free forever, and **no competitor can ever have it** — which is the moat argument in [02](02-positioning.md) applied to imagery. Also a natural content story in its own right.

### Route C · Shoot your own "old photograph"

The black-and-white image in the reference is, mechanically, a grainy profile with one object in colour. **That is a look, not an artefact — you can shoot it.**

Real black-and-white (film or a proper conversion), hard side light, profile, heavy grain, the piece selectively in colour. A model fee and half a day, and you own it permanently. It is also **frame 3 from [08](08-brand.md) doing its job properly**: a real ear, real scale, real drape — so it earns its place in the grid instead of costing you the returns that a faked scale causes.

This is the clearest illustration of the whole document: the reference's most striking image required nothing it did not have. It stole out of convenience.

### Route D · The Tokyo frame — the one that is strategically better

The reference borrows European interiors **because it has no real world of its own.** Hikari does.

A grainy 35mm frame of the actual shop, the actual drawer of unsorted pieces, hands, a Tokyo back room in the morning — with one piece sharp in the foreground. Same four mechanisms: texture, time, human presence, one sharp object. But it is **documentary, owned outright, and literally impossible for a UK competitor to reproduce**. It is [05](05-social.md)'s pillar 2, which is already identified as the only pillar nobody else in the category has.

*Caution:* this is not a licence to reach for ukiyo-e or Japanese motif as backdrop. Much of it is public domain and it would be the easy move, but [08](08-brand.md)'s rule holds — **the Japanese reference must never become costume.** A real room in Tokyo is provenance. A woodblock print behind an earring is decoration, and the wrong kind.

## 6 · Recommendation

**Run A and D together, and shoot rather than composite in both cases.**

A gives the borrowed-painting register for the seduction frames, with a documented licence and an image you own. D gives the atmosphere posts [05](05-social.md) identified as the reference's real strength, sourced from the one thing this business has that the reference does not. C is the upgrade to frame 3 whenever there is a shoot day. B is worth one commission once there is budget, for the Alcove.

Skip AI backdrops. The cost is not legal — it is that the brand's entire argument is *this object is real, and here is where it was found*.

**And whichever route: the hard line from [D8](DECISIONS.md) stands. Never composite a piece onto an image the business did not make.** Route A is not a loophole in that rule — it complies with it, because the photograph is yours.

## Update · 2026-09-22 — the hybrid, and how to produce it

**Owner's call, recorded: a generated backdrop is adopted in a narrowed form.** §3 above recommended against it and that reasoning is unchanged and still worth re-reading before this scales. What was chosen is **narrower than what §3 rejected**, and the narrowing matters:

> **The backdrop is generated. The piece is photographed.** Only the environment is synthetic; the object remains a real photograph of the real item.

This keeps the argument in §3's last paragraph mostly intact. The claim *"real object, real age, found in Tokyo"* is not contradicted by a painted setting any more than a studio sweep contradicts it — **what would contradict it is a synthetic piece**, and that is not what is being made. The evidence objection in [D8](DECISIONS.md) is likewise satisfied: true scale and condition survive, because the earring in frame is the earring being sold.

**The ownership caveat in §3 is not resolved by the narrowing and stands in full.** Generated output may carry no copyright anyone can enforce. *Commercial use* is generally permitted by the generating service's terms — **check the specific service's terms, they differ and they change** — but *exclusivity* is a separate question, and the answer may be that there isn't any. Budget for the possibility that a competitor copies the feed and there is no claim to bring.

### The production rule that decides whether this works

**Generate the sitter with a bare ear. Never generate the earring.**

Removing or overpainting a generated earring is far harder than dropping a real one onto a clean lobe, and every attempt leaves the artefacts that make composites read as cheap ([05](05-social.md)). A bare, well-lit, unobstructed ear is the entire output requirement.

### Two register specifications

Both are written to Hikari's palette rather than the reference's warm brocade, which is the point of specifying them here at all. Neither names a living artist, a named photographer's style or a real person — §3's second risk is a prompt risk, and it is avoided at the prompt.

**A · The painted sitter.** East Asian woman, three-quarter turn away from camera, hair up, one ear fully visible and bare. Oil on canvas with visible brushwork and canvas tooth. Cool muted ground — grey-green, `銀鼠`, deep indigo in the shadows — with the skin warm against it. Soft directional north light. Plain dark neutral background, shallow depth, negative space to one side. No jewellery of any kind. 4:5.

**B · The old photograph.** East Asian woman in strict profile, hair slicked into a low knot, ear fully exposed and bare. Black and white, medium format, heavy silver grain, hard side light, deep blacks and a clean highlight roll-off. Plain pale grey seamless. No jewellery. 4:5.

B is the more valuable of the two: it is mechanically what the reference's strongest image is, and [11](11-imagery-rights.md) route C shoots it for real as soon as there is a shoot day. **Treat the generated version as a stand-in for route C, not as its replacement.**

### Compositing — the five things that decide if it reads as real

1. **Match the light direction first.** Read where the light falls in the generated frame, then shoot the piece under the same direction and quality. Nothing else on this list matters if this is wrong, and it is the single most common tell.
2. **Add a contact shadow** where the piece meets the lobe. A floating earring is the second most common tell.
3. **Grain goes on last, over everything.** Composite first, grain the flattened image second — the exact point [08](08-brand.md) makes about grain over image and type together. Grain applied only to the backdrop leaves the piece sitting visibly on top of it.
4. **In register B, pull the piece's colour toward the photograph.** Keep it in colour, but desaturate slightly and match its white point to the frame's, or it detaches.
5. **Check the scale against a real ear.** Frame 3 exists to prevent scale-driven returns ([08](08-brand.md)); a composite that gets this wrong causes exactly the returns the frame was meant to stop. Measure, don't eyeball.

### One open decision — the recurring sitter

A fixed seed and a fixed prompt produce the same face every Edition, which over a year becomes a brand asset in the way *The Alcove* is ([08](08-brand.md)).

It also **sharpens the AI tell considerably**: a face that recurs but never ages, never appears in Stories and never has a name invites exactly the scrutiny §3 warns about. The safer pattern is a *painted* recurring sitter (register A), where a consistent face is a convention of portraiture rather than an anomaly, and one-off faces in register B.
