# 03 — Customer

> **The question asked:** at £100–300, do people actually have the money?

## Short answer

Yes — and the evidence is unusually direct. **£100–300 is the normal band for a considered UK jewellery purchase, not a stretch one.**

But the framing of the question needs correcting, because it points at the wrong risk. At this price point in this category, **the barrier is not affordability. It is trust.** The customer can find £180. What she cannot easily do is believe that an unknown website will send her a genuine, accurately-described, thirty-year-old object, and take it back if it isn't. Every pound of effort spent on making the price feel smaller is worth less than a pound spent on making the seller feel safer.

---

## The affordability evidence

**The UK jewellery and watch market grew 3.6% in 2025 to £6.41bn** ([Mintel](https://store.mintel.com/report/uk-jewellery-watches-retailing-market-report)). It is forecast to recover slowly but consistently through 2026–2032 as economic pressure eases, with affordability remaining a live factor in the buying decision ([Vyansa](https://www.vyansaintelligence.com/industry-report/uk-jewellery-market-analysis)).

**The single most useful figure found:** Mother's Day 2026 led UK spending at £7.5bn, with 45% of consumers planning to buy jewellery for a loved one, and **the average shopper expecting to spend a record £284.25** ([Classy Women Collection, 2026](https://classywomencollection.com/blogs/fashion-guide/jewelry-market)).

That number sits at the *top* of the Hikari band. A £180 piece is comfortably inside what a normal UK consumer already plans to spend on jewellery for one gifting occasion. The price point is validated by existing behaviour, not by hope.

**Two supporting signals:**

- **Married 25–34-year-old women on an average salary buy the most jewellery** — a tighter and more accessible target than "affluent women", and one that maps onto exactly the demographic most reachable through Instagram and TikTok.
- **Self-gift jewellery sales have grown 58% since 2021.** This matters more than it looks. It means the £100–300 purchase no longer needs an occasion attached to it. Occasion-only demand is seasonal, low-frequency and hard to build a business on; self-gifting is year-round and repeatable.

**The wider current runs the right way.** Global luxury resale is projected at $41.61bn in 2026, up from $37.95bn in 2025 — around 9.6% growth ([TBRC](https://www.thebusinessresearchcompany.com/report/luxury-resale-global-market-report)). European secondhand luxury was $19.3bn in 2024 and is forecast to roughly double by 2033. Resale is growing several times faster than primary retail, and consumers are explicitly trading into it as a compromise between cost and desire ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/luxury-resale-on-rise-consumers-compromise-between-cost-comfort)).

**The counterweight, stated honestly:** price remains the main purchasing driver for most UK consumers, and many are actively choosing value brands and lower-cost retailers to stretch their money. This does not invalidate £100–300, but it does mean Hikari must be *legible as good value* and not merely as expensive-and-beautiful. Which leads to the most important point on this page.

## The comparison set is the whole argument

The customer with £180 in hand is not choosing between Hikari and another vintage seller. She is choosing between Hikari and **a new piece from the demi-fine mid-market** — Missoma, Monica Vinader, Astrid & Miyu, Otiumberg — which occupies almost exactly the same £80–250 band and spends heavily to own it.

Put side by side, at £180:

| | New demi-fine | Hikari |
|---|---|---|
| Uniqueness | Thousands made, restocked | **One. Nobody else has it.** |
| Design provenance | In-house studio, this season | **A Paris couture house, 1985** |
| Craft | Contemporary mass production | **Hand-set, made when costume jewellery was still made properly** |
| Resale value | Falls to near zero | **Holds or appreciates** |
| Sustainability | New materials, new supply chain | **Nothing extracted, nothing manufactured** |
| Availability | Order any time | Gone when it's gone |

**This is the strongest value story in the business and it should be argued explicitly on the site** — not left implicit. For the same money as a new chain from a brand with a heavy paid-social budget, you get a Christian Dior piece from 1985 that no one else in the room will be wearing.

*Verify the current price bands of these four brands before publishing any comparison — they move.*

## Who she is

Three segments. Each needs different pages, different copy and a different acquisition channel.

### A — The fashion-literate self-gifter · *the core, ~55% of revenue*
28–40. London, Manchester, Bristol, Glasgow, Brighton. Professional, design-aware, probably already buys secondhand clothing without thinking of it as thrift. Discovers through Instagram and TikTok. Buys **£120–220**, roughly 2–4 times a year.

She is not looking for a bargain and she is not a collector. She wants an object with a story that signals taste rather than spend. **She buys the photograph.** Her blocker is "is this site real?" — which is answered by consistent photography, a named human founder, visible returns terms and social proof.

### B — The collector · *smaller, ~25% of revenue, higher AOV*
45–65. Knows the makers by name — Attwood & Sawyer, Trifari, Grosse, Schiaparelli, Napier. Searches by maker, decade and signature rather than browsing. Buys **£200–400+**, and buys repeatedly once she trusts you.

**She buys the description, not the photograph.** She wants the signature mark, the period attribution, the exact condition, the construction detail. Thin listings lose her instantly. She is also the cheapest customer to acquire — she is already searching for "vintage Dior earrings" on Google — and the most loyal. Serve her with long-form listings and a real authentication page. She will forgive a plain website; she will not forgive a vague one.

### C — The gifter · *seasonal, ~20% of revenue, concentrated in Q4*
Any age, frequently male, buying for someone else. Buys **£100–200**. Needs: gift framing, a "gifts under £150" collection, gift wrap, a gift receipt, a clear delivery cut-off date, and — critically — **an easy exchange path**, because he is guessing.

The Mother's Day figure above (£284.25 average intended spend) is segment C's number. There are two hard peaks to plan inventory around: **Christmas and Mothering Sunday.**

## The real barrier, and how to remove it

Trust, in the order the customer meets it:

1. **"Is this genuine?"** → a proper authentication page explaining *how* you assess — signature marks, construction, clasp type, plating — not just an "authenticated" badge. Publish the method. Jagged Metal ranks on this content precisely because it does both jobs at once.
2. **"What does 'vintage condition' actually mean?"** → a published condition grading scale, applied to every single listing, with the honest wear photograph. Consistency here is worth more than generosity.
3. **"What if it's wrong when it arrives?"** → **14-day right to cancel, stated plainly.** Note: under the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 this is a legal obligation for UK distance selling, not a gesture. Since you must offer it, make it loud and take the marketing credit. *Confirm exact obligations — including who bears return postage — with an adviser.*
4. **"Who am I buying from?"** → a founder, a face, a reason. "Independent" only converts if someone is visibly behind it.
5. **"Can I spread the cost?"** → Klarna/Clearpay does real work between £150 and £300; it reliably shifts basket size upward in this band. **UK BNPL regulation is moving — confirm current FCA requirements and any disclosure obligations before switching it on.**
6. **"Will I get a surprise customs bill?"** → *No.* Stock is in the UK, duty paid. **Say this on the site.** It is the direct answer to every Japan-based competitor, who ships from Tokyo and leaves the buyer to be met at the door by a courier's invoice.
