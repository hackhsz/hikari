# 02 — Positioning

> **The question asked:** independent-curated, or mass-market?

## Recommendation

**Independent-curated, on an owned storefront — decisively.** Marketplaces are an acquisition and liquidation channel, never the home.

But with an important qualification that the word "independent" usually hides: *independent taste, mass-market execution.* The common failure of small vintage sellers is treating "indie" as permission to have a slow site, vague condition notes, no returns policy and erratic photography. Be idiosyncratic about what you buy and utterly conventional about how you sell it.

---

## Why not mass

Four reasons, in order of how much they matter.

**1. The arithmetic forbids it.** Mass-market means competing on assortment and price. Assortment is impossible: one-of-one stock, hand-sourced, means tens of new SKUs a month, not thousands. Price is self-defeating: marketplace commission on a £180 item eats a large share of the gross margin on a piece that cost £55–70 to land. You would be doing the hardest part of the job — going to Japan and having taste — and handing the reward to a platform.

**2. The price frame is wrong.** Vestiaire's own headline is *"up to 70% off retail prices."* That frame asks the customer: what is the cheapest way to get a designer name? Hikari's answer to that question is bad — the original retail price of a 1988 Dior earring is unknowable and irrelevant. The frame that works is: *this object is beautiful, it is thirty years old, there is one of it, and it costs £180.* That frame cannot survive on a page that puts it beside a discount badge.

**3. The moat is on the supply side and only a brand can capture it.** The hard, defensible thing here is access — Yahoo Auctions, Komehyo, Brand-Off, the Tokyo reuse chain. A UK customer cannot easily do this herself. But "we go to Japan" is a *story*, and stories have no surface on a marketplace listing. On a marketplace you are an anonymous SKU with a seller rating. Same goods, none of the premium.

**4. The upside path requires a brand.** Susan Caplan's business became valuable when her curation could be sold *into* Goldsmiths, Mappin & Webb, Harvey Nichols and Net-a-Porter. Wholesale, licensing, collaborations, a physical concession — every one of those futures requires a name. None of them is available to a top-rated eBay seller.

## Where marketplaces still earn their place

Not zero. Used deliberately:

- **eBay / Etsy as a discovery tap.** High-intent search traffic you cannot buy cheaply otherwise. List a *subset* — never the best pieces — at a **10–15% premium** to the site price to absorb fees, with packaging and inserts that drive the buyer to the owned site. Treat it as paid acquisition that happens to be profitable.
- **Vestiaire / Depop for liquidation.** Anything unsold after 90 days. Cash recovery, not brand-building.
- **Never** put the hero pieces, the new-in drop, or anything with a strong story on a marketplace first. The drop belongs to the email list.

Rough shape to aim for by month 12: **~70% owned site · ~20% marketplaces · ~10% wholesale/events.** A number to test, not a target to defend.

## The aesthetic position

This is the more interesting half of the question, and the research turned up a genuine gap.

### What the category currently looks like

Every serious player in vintage designer jewellery runs some version of the same visual idea: **French luxury restraint.** Black type on white, high-contrast serif headings, generous margins, a product shot on seamless white. Susan Caplan, Bujor, Jagged Metal, ReSee — the differences are matters of degree. It is a good language. It is also, at this point, wallpaper.

Meanwhile the broader e-commerce design conversation in 2026 is pulling toward **editorial full-bleed** — [Staud](https://www.staud.clothing/)'s magazine-like layouts and playful prop-led product photography, [Bottega Veneta](https://www.bottegaveneta.com/)'s silent auto-looping runway film as a hero with no UI chrome on top of it, [Allbirds](https://www.allbirds.com/)' warm-toned material-led calm. The direction of travel is *away* from the product grid and *toward* the page that looks like it was art-directed.

### The gap

**Nobody in vintage designer resale is using a Japanese visual identity — despite Japan being where an enormous share of the world's vintage supply actually comes from.** Everyone borrows Paris because the *brands* are Parisian. But Hikari's differentiator is not that it sells Dior; a dozen sites sell Dior. It is *where the Dior comes from*.

### The proposal: Japanese editorial, not French luxury

A visual language taken from Japanese retail and print design rather than European fashion houses:

- **Space as the luxury signal**, not gold. Large empty fields, small type, a lot of air. Japanese editorial design is confident about emptiness in a way European luxury is not.
- **Asymmetric, modular grids.** Product tiles at varying scale rather than an even matrix — the page reads as composed, not generated.
- **Paper tones over pure white.** Warm off-whites, ink-black, a single muted accent. Not gold, not blush.
- **Typography:** a restrained grotesque for interface, one high-quality serif reserved for names and numbers. Resist Japanese-styled display faces and anything with a brush or "zen" affect — the reference is *Kinfolk*, *Casa Brutus*, Muji's catalogues and Ginza department-store packaging, not a ramen shop sign. **The Japanese reference must never become costume.**
- **The name does the rest.** *Hikari* — light. Photograph everything in real daylight, with real shadows, and the concept and the craft are the same decision.

### Photography, which matters more than the layout

At £100–300 on a secondhand object, photography *is* conversion. Four frames per piece, non-negotiable:

1. **The composed shot** — the piece on a paper ground in daylight. This is the one that goes on Instagram.
2. **The macro** — the signature/maker's mark, the clasp, the stone setting. This is the authentication evidence and it doubles as the most compelling content.
3. **On-body** — scale. Statement vintage earrings are usually much larger than people imagine; this single frame prevents a large share of returns.
4. **The honest condition frame** — the wear, plated loss, a missing stone, shown clearly. Counter-intuitively this *raises* conversion: it proves the other three photos are not hiding anything.

An aesthetic that flatters the object at the cost of hiding its condition will generate returns, disputes and bad reviews. Beauty and disclosure have to be the same photograph set.

## The one-line positioning

> **Hikari finds vintage Dior, Chanel and Givenchy in Japan, and brings it to the UK — authenticated, duty paid, next day.**

Each clause is a competitive claim. *Japan* is the supply moat. *Authenticated* is the trust barrier. *Duty paid, next day* is the direct attack on every Japan-based seller, who ships slowly and leaves the customer to be ambushed by a courier's customs invoice at the door.

## Open

- Does the Japan sourcing story move a UK buyer, or is it founder-romance? **Test it: run the first 20 sales with the story foregrounded in half the listings and absent in the other half.**
- Is "Japanese editorial" legible as premium to a UK mass audience, or does it read as under-designed? Worth a cheap test — two landing-page variants before any full build.
