Marks built on 光

Yes — Japanese writes it with exactly this character. 光 is a jōyō kanji, six strokes, and it was never given a separate simplified form: the Japanese, simplified-Chinese and traditional-Chinese characters are the same character. But the drawn proportions are not the same, and the difference falls precisely on the two strokes we care about.

The same character, drawn four ways

All four below are the identical code point set in that region's own serif. Look only at the two top strokes and the air beneath them.

Japan · 明朝
光

Short top strokes, clear air above the bar. The most compact top of the four — and the one that leaves room for something to hang there.

PRC · 宋体
光

Longer top strokes reaching down toward the bar; the gap nearly closes. A taller, tighter top.

Taiwan · 明體
光

Long strokes again, with a straighter left stroke. Closest to the PRC form, not to the Japanese one.

Korea · 명조
光

Between the two. Hanja are set from a third set of conventions again.

Two consequences, and both are practical. First: set 光 in a Chinese font on a brand whose whole story is Japanese sourcing and a Japanese eye will find it subtly wrong — the kind of detail a Tokyo dealer notices and a London customer never does. Specify the Japanese form, in writing. Second, and more useful: the Japanese convention is the one that leaves air under the top strokes — which is exactly the space a pair of drops needs. The regional form we should be using is also the form that makes the idea work.

Ground

Five simpler constructions

All built from the same six-stroke skeleton, no font involved. Each shown large, then at 16px.

01 · 丸に光
The ring
16px

The kamon enclosure — the device Mitsubishi and Kikkoman still use. A ring gives a mark stability and a Japanese read without a motif. Keep it a hairline ring, never a filled disc.

02 · 陰刻
Struck in
16px

One shape with a hole in it. The simplest silhouette available and the best performer at 16px, because the square carries the weight and the character only has to interrupt it.

03 · 界線
The shared rule
16px

The character's horizontal is the page's hairline rule. The mark supplies five short strokes; the layout supplies the sixth and completes it. Minimal by construction, and it makes the logo part of the grid rather than an object sitting on it.

04 · 方眼
Modular
16px

Every stroke on one grid, two lengths only, terminals square-cut. Simple in the systematic sense — but the symmetric top ticks push it toward a radiating form. Watch that.

05 · 細線
Hairline
16px

Lighter looks simpler, and it is the trap. Turn the grain on. It also cannot be stamped, embossed or embroidered, and the 16px version is already a smudge.

06 · 滴
Drops · current
16px

Where we got to last time, for comparison. Not simpler than these — more specific. The drops are the only element in the whole set that is about jewellery.

Recommendation

03 as the idea, 02 as the stamp, 06 for what goes in them

These are not six competitors. Three of them combine, and the two to avoid are the ones that look simplest.

  1. 03 — the shared rule — is the real answer to "simpler". It is the only one that gets simpler by removing a stroke from the logo and giving it to the page. It suits a brand already built on hairlines and empty space, and it makes the mark part of the grid instead of an object placed on it. It is also the most defensible, because it is a behaviour competitors cannot copy by tracing a shape.
  2. 02 — struck in — is the stamp and the favicon. Best silhouette, best at 16px, and it is literally the intaglio state already specified for the punch.
  3. Keep the drops (06) as what sits in them. Simple and generic is easy; simple and specific is the job. Everything on this page except the drops could belong to any business called 光.
  4. Avoid 05 entirely, and watch 04. Hairline weight looks like restraint and fails grain, stamping, embroidery and 16px at once. The modular version's symmetric top ticks drift toward a radiating sun — the standing prohibition applies.
  5. Specify the Japanese glyph form in writing. Whatever is drawn, its proportions come from the 明朝 convention, not from 宋体. A Tokyo dealer will see the difference; the brief should name it.
