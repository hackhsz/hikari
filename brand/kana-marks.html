The name in kana, and five ways to get simpler

One fact first, because it changes what the mark is allowed to be: 光 on its own does not reliably say "Hikari." Its on-reading is コウ kō; its kun-readings are ひかり hikari and ひかる hikaru; as a name it is also read みつ, てる, あきら and more. A Japanese reader meeting the character cold is as likely to say kō or hikaru.

That is not fatal — the Latin wordmark carries the name, so the mark never has to. But it removes one argument for the kanji. If we want the Japanese side of the identity to say the name — on a supplier's invoice, a Tokyo business card, a packing slip going back to a dealer — that job belongs to kana, not to 光.

The four ways to write it

光
kanji · kō / hikari

Intellectual, sincere, trustworthy — and stiff. Carries the meaning, not the sound. One character, so the densest and the best in a square.

ひかり
hiragana · hikari

Soft and approachable, and the only writing that says the name unambiguously. Three curved characters — the hardest of the four to compress.

ヒカリ
katakana · hikari

Light, modern, and the easiest to arrange — its base forms are the simplest in the language. Six straight strokes, no curves. The script built for this job.

HIKARI
latin · the wordmark

Already decided. It carries the name for the people buying. Everything on this page is the mark beside it, never a replacement.

Katakana is the answer to "simpler" if the answer is typographic. It is described in Japanese design writing exactly this way: lighter and more contemporary than kanji, and easier to arrange because its letterforms are already simple. Hiragana is warmer but curvier; kanji is denser but stiffer and, here, ambiguous.

Ground

Six marks, simplest last

Each shown large, then at 16px beside it. Nothing here is more than one colour.

01
光, drawn · drops
16px

The synthesis. Four parts, no font, entirely ours — the constructed 光 with the drop pair in place of the two top strokes. Simple and specific.

02
ヒカリ · 縦組
16px

Six straight strokes in a column. Composition, not motif — which is exactly the rule the brand set. Says the name. Fails at 16px.

03
ひ · one stroke
16px

A single continuous form, and the first sound of the name. The most beautiful of the six — and it says hi, not hikari.

04
ひかり · 横組
16px

The softest, and the only one that unambiguously says the name in Japanese. A second wordmark rather than a mark — no square use at all.

05
The pair alone
16px

Two shapes and nothing else. Survives anything — 16px, grain, a stamp, embroidery. Says earrings and light; says no language at all.

06
光, drawn · plain
16px

The previous recommendation, for comparison. Simple, but nothing in it is about jewellery — it could belong to any business called Hikari.

Recommendation

01 for the mark, ヒカリ as the Japanese signature, ひかり nowhere

"Simpler" and "more distinctive" usually pull against each other. Here they do not, because the drops are the one simplification that also adds meaning.

  1. 01 is the mark. Four parts, no font, no borrowed glyph — and the two drops are the only element on this page that is about jewellery rather than about Japan. 06 is simpler still and says nothing; that is the whole argument.
  2. 05 is the fallback, and the better one if this has to get smaller. Two shapes survive embroidery, hot foil, a 16px favicon and a bad printer. Keep it as the monogram inside the punch when the full mark will not fit.
  3. ヒカリ, vertical, is the Japanese-facing signature — on the Tokyo business card, the supplier paperwork, the box tape. Katakana because it is the script built to be arranged, and vertical because a column is composition rather than decoration.
  4. Drop ひかり and ひ. ひ is the most beautiful object here and says one syllable; ひかり is three curves that will not compress. Neither earns a slot the Latin wordmark already fills.
  5. Nothing changes about what gates this. Trade mark clearance in Classes 14 and 35 comes before a designer is briefed.
