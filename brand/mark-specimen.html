Four ways the mark could go

Palette B · 銀鼠 Newsreader + Instrument Sans Specimen, not artwork

Every competitor in the category uses a plain text logo and nothing else — Susan Caplan, Jagged Metal and ReSee are all wordmark-only. So the open question is not which typeface. It is whether Hikari has a mark at all, and what that mark has to survive.

The grain eats hairlines

D6 puts animated film grain over the entire page, type included. A fine-stroke mark does not survive it at small size. Turn the grain on below and watch direction 4 disappear.

The ground moves

Background colour is a function of scroll position — 白練 → 銀鼠 → 藍 → 濡羽色. The mark must be one colour, no two-tone, no white knockout, legible on all four.

An abstract mark is a weak mark

Overly simple or abstract devices get refused as trade marks for lacking distinctive character. The more generic the shape, the less of it is defensible in Class 14.

Ground
01

The struck mark

刻印

A punch: a closed shape with something struck inside it. This is the shared grammar of the sponsor's mark on the back of a piece of silver, the 落款印 an artist presses into a painting to authenticate it, and the 家紋 enclosure that Mitsubishi and Kikkoman both still use. Hikari's second product is authentication — and frame 2 of the photography spec is already a macro of somebody else's maker's mark.

For

  • Holds at 16px, which no wordmark does.
  • Concept and category coincide: the business sells marks on the backs of objects.
  • A device with a closed contour and a struck interior is registrable in a way a bare shape is not.
  • Physical from day one — a rubber stamp on the mailer is the cheapest premium signal available.

Against

  • Initials-in-a-cartouche is the antiques trade's own cliché. It has to be drawn as a modern punch, not an engraved crest.
  • Needs a decision on the interior before anything else can be drawn.

Hard legal constraint. Under the Hallmarking Act 1973 it is an offence to strike an article with a mark purporting to be an authorised sponsor's mark, and an assay office may obliterate any mark it considers likely to be confused with a hallmark. So: never struck into metal, only into paper — and the outline avoids the shield and lozenge shapes the 1973 punch regulations use. A rounded square (the 角印 logic) is clear of them.

02

The wordmark that never grows

白練

No device at all. HIKARI set small, uppercase, widely tracked — the same 0.12em label treatment already specified for CHRISTIAN DIOR · c.1987 · GRADE II. The differentiation is not the shape, it is the behaviour: the logo is never larger than the piece, ever, on any surface. The tokonoma rule applied to the brand's own name.

For

  • Costs nothing and can ship this week.
  • Nothing to get wrong — no motif, no borrowed symbol, no costume.
  • A word mark in plain type is the strongest thing to own in Class 14, stronger than any device.

Against

  • There is no asset for the favicon, the avatar, the stamp or the seal — the three squares above are all failures.
  • Identical in kind to all three competitors. Restraint you cannot see is not positioning.
03

The bilingual lockup

光 / HIKARI

The kanji carries the compact slots, the Latin carries the name. A kanji is designed to resolve inside a square at small size, which is exactly what a favicon and an avatar are. It also states the sourcing without a sentence of copy.

For

  • The best small-size performance of the four, by a distance.
  • Honest to the sourcing, and nobody in the category has it.
  • Gives the name a face for a UK buyer who has never seen the word before.

Against

  • Runs straight at the brand's own rule: the Japanese reference must never become costume. A kanji is a motif in a way that letter-spacing is not.
  • A lone kanji on a UK site is also the single most-borrowed Japanese signifier there is.

The difference between this reading as provenance and reading as costume is almost entirely where it is allowed to appear. Inside a square that needs filling — avatar, favicon, stamp, the seal on a care card — it is a mark. Set large across a homepage above English copy, it is a costume. That is a system rule, not a drawing problem.

04

The alcove

床の間

The one direction that draws the name rather than the letters: light entering a recess. An upright opening, the light falling across it, nothing else. It is the tokonoma principle as a shape, and it is the direction a designer will reach for first.

For

  • Draws the concept the whole site is already built on, and the name at the same time.
  • No borrowed motif to get wrong.

Against

  • A gradient is not a mark. It cannot be stamped, embossed, foiled or faxed, and it dies under grain — turn the grain on.
  • An upright rectangle is close to unregistrable: too simple and too decorative to carry distinctive character.
  • At 16px it is a rectangle. Every brand with a rectangle is this brand.
—

And the one to reject out loud

No mark at all — the Muji move. 無印良品 means "no-mark quality goods", and the argument for it is genuinely the same argument this brand already makes about empty space. It does not transfer. Muji can decline to have a mark because it is already one of the most recognised brands on earth; the absence reads as confidence because everyone knows whose absence it is. An unknown seller asking a stranger for £180 has no such stock of recognition to spend. Here the absence would read as an absence.

Recommendation

01 as the mark, 02 as the logo, 03 borrowed for the interior

They are not four competing logos. Three of them are one system, and the fourth is the one to drop.

  1. The primary logo is the wordmark (02). Tracked uppercase, small, and governed by a size rule rather than a size spec: never larger than the piece on the page.
  2. The compact mark is a struck punch (01) — and it earns its place by doing the four jobs the wordmark provably fails: favicon, avatar, the stamp on the mailer, the seal on the care card.
  3. What goes inside the punch is the one real open question. 光 (03) performs best and carries the most risk; H in Newsreader is safe and generic. This is the thing to test, and it is a smaller and cheaper question than "what should the logo be".
  4. Drop the alcove (04). It is the most attractive drawing here and the least usable mark. Keep the idea — it already exists as the homepage feature, which is where it belongs.
