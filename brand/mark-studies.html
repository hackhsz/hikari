How far to push the 光

Direction: 光 is the body of the mark, but it should not sit on the page as a character someone typed. That instinct is right, and it is the difference between a device you own and a glyph you borrowed. The whole question becomes: how far, and where does it stop being 光.

Three things it fixes at once. It answers the costume objection — a drawn abstraction reads as a mark first and a character second, which is exactly the "restraint, never motif" rule from 08-brand.md. It answers registrability — a dictionary glyph set in a licensed font is weak as a device, while a distinctively constructed one is a real mark. And it answers 16px, because abstraction is mostly the act of removing parts.

What makes 光 unusually good for this. Structurally it is three marks radiating above a horizontal bar, standing on two legs. Which is to say the character already draws light falling onto a surface — the tokonoma, in six strokes. Very few characters carry their meaning as usable geometry. This one does.

Ground

The ladder

Five steps from typed to unreadable. Each is the same skeleton with one more thing taken away.

00 · Typed
明朝

A text face, doing a text face's job. Modulated strokes, calligraphic terminals, optimised for 11px body copy. You own none of it.

01 · Drawn
Monolinear

Same skeleton, one stroke weight, flat terminals, no calligraphic memory. Already an object rather than a setting.

02 · Constructed
Regularised

Ticks to one shared angle, bar to full width, hook flick dropped, feet on one baseline. Unmistakably 光, unmistakably drawn.

03 · Reduced
Four parts

The ticks detach and equalise; the bar takes over; the legs shorten and mirror. Now it reads as light over a surface — and still resolves as 光.

04 · Too far
Not 光 any more

Drop the legs and it is a sunrise over a line — a form so common it is close to unownable, and it has stopped saying the name.

The answer is between 02 and 03, and which one is a question about how much the mark has to do at 16px, not a question of taste. Everything below tests exactly that.

Three ways this goes wrong

Radiating rays

Never radial, never from a disc, never more than the three strokes the character actually has. A rayed sun is the 旭日旗, and in a brand sourcing from Japan and selling internationally that is not a subtle association — it is a live crisis that has hit fashion labels repeatedly. The three ticks of 光 are asymmetric and that asymmetry is the safeguard. Keep it.

The middle zone

Half-regularised is the worst place to stand: too tidy to be handwriting, too irregular to be construction. It does not read as a designed mark — it reads as a character drawn by someone who cannot write it. Commit to 02 or 03; do not stop between them.

Six parts at 16px

Six strokes and two counters inside 16 pixels, under grain, is too much information. Abstraction here is not a style — it is the mechanism that gets the part-count down. That is why step 03 exists and why the hook flick goes first.

Where this lands

Constructed 光, two states, one size rule

  1. Draw it, never set it. Monolinear, one stroke weight, flat terminals, feet on one baseline, the three ticks sharing a single angle. The angle of those ticks is where the entire personality of this brand's mark lives — it is the one thing worth iterating on properly.
  2. Two states, borrowed from the trade: cameo and intaglio. Positive on the site, knocked out of a filled punch for the stamp and the seal. Same artwork.
  3. Hold the reduction at four visual parts — ticks, bar, two legs. Anything that survives 16px under grain survives everything else.
  4. Test it on people who read the character. The one question we cannot answer ourselves: does a Japanese reader still see 光 without hesitating? A hesitation means the middle zone. That is a five-minute test and it decides between 02 and 03.
  5. The alcove comes back. Step 03 draws light falling on a horizontal surface — which is the tokonoma, now stampable. The abstract direction that was dropped for being undrawable returns here as structure.
